Passive Voice: What It Is and How to Fix It

By Editorial Team • 6 Min Read

Every writing teacher, editor, and style guide seems to agree on one rule: avoid passive voice. But most writers can't actually define it, which makes it almost impossible to fix. Here's what passive voice really is, how to spot it in your own writing, and when — despite its reputation — it's actually the better choice.

What Passive Voice Actually Is

In an active sentence, the subject performs the action: "The chef cooked the meal." In a passive sentence, the subject receives the action instead: "The meal was cooked by the chef." The grammatical signature of passive voice is a form of the verb "to be" (is, was, were, been, being) followed by a past participle — usually a verb ending in "-ed," like written, made, or completed.

Why Editors Flag It So Often

Passive voice isn't grammatically wrong, but it usually makes writing weaker in three specific ways:

  • It's longer. "The report was written by Sarah" is seven words; "Sarah wrote the report" is four. Passive constructions almost always add extra words without adding meaning.
  • It hides responsibility. "Mistakes were made" is a classic non-apology precisely because it never says who made them. Passive voice lets writers quietly omit the actor.
  • It reads as less direct. Active sentences put the doer front and center, which tends to feel more confident and easier to follow.

Before and After: Five Real Examples

✗ The ball was thrown by the pitcher.

✓ The pitcher threw the ball.

✗ A decision was made by the committee to delay the launch.

✓ The committee decided to delay the launch.

✗ Errors in the code were identified by the QA team.

✓ The QA team identified errors in the code.

✗ The award was given to her for outstanding service.

✓ She received the award for outstanding service.

✗ The new policy was announced by management on Monday.

✓ Management announced the new policy on Monday.

When Passive Voice Is Actually the Right Call

Passive voice isn't always a mistake. It's the better choice when:

  • The actor is unknown or irrelevant: "My car was stolen last night" — you don't know who did it, so naming an actor isn't possible.
  • The action matters more than who did it: "The bridge was completed in 1937" reads more naturally than naming the specific construction crew.
  • You're writing in a scientific or technical register: "The sample was heated to 80°C" is standard in lab reports, where the process matters more than the person performing it.

The goal isn't to eliminate passive voice entirely — it's to notice when you're using it by default rather than by choice.
